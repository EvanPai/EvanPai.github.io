Predicting the compute behind an AI model, without leaking the answer

Python
machine learning
scaling laws
data analysis
Training compute for notable AI models doubles about every six months. I measure the trend with bootstrap intervals, test the C ≈ 6ND rule, then build a leakage-aware model validated on models released after its training cut-off, and find where it breaks.
Author

Evan Pai

Published

September 22, 2026

Labs rarely say how much compute a new model took. They do often say how many parameters it has, roughly how much data it saw, and who built it. This post asks two questions. How fast is training compute growing? And how well can it be estimated from the details that are public? The second one is a small but realistic ML problem. The labels are noisy, a third of the features are missing, two tempting columns leak the answer, and the model has to hold up on models released after the training data ends.

How fast is training compute growing?

I regress log10(compute) on publication year. The slope \(b\) is in orders of magnitude per year, so the doubling time is \(\log_{10} 2 / b\) years. The data are uneven (many models from 2020 onwards, few from early years) and the residuals are far from normal. So instead of trusting the textbook standard error, I bootstrap: refit on 2,000 resampled datasets and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the doubling time.

def doubling_months(df):
    slope = np.polyfit(df["year"], np.log10(df["compute"]), 1)[0]
    return 12 * np.log10(2) / slope

def bootstrap(df, n=2000):
    idx = rng.integers(0, len(df), size=(n, len(df)))
    return np.array([doubling_months(df.iloc[i]) for i in idx])

subsets = {
    "All domains": dl,
    "Language": dl[dl["Domain"] == "Language"],
    "Vision": dl[dl["Domain"] == "Vision"],
}
rows = []
for name, df in subsets.items():

                 "doubling time (months)": round(doubling_months(df), 1),

Compute doubles roughly every six months. That is about three times faster than the hardware trend in my memory-wall post, where peak FLOP/s per chip doubles about every 1.6 years. Most of the growth therefore comes from using more chips for longer, not from faster chips.

Does C ≈ 6ND hold?

For dense transformers, training compute is often approximated as \(C \approx 6ND\): \(N\) parameters, \(D\) training tokens, and 6 FLOPs per parameter per token (2 for the forward pass, 4 for the backward pass). If that holds, the two public numbers alone would almost pin down the answer. I check it on the language models that report all three quantities.

lang = dl[(dl["Domain"] == "Language") & dl["params"].notna() & dl["tokens"].notna()]
ratio = np.log10(lang["compute"] / (6 * lang["params"] * lang["tokens"]))

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 3.6))
ax.hist(ratio, bins=40, color="#1b6ca8", alpha=0.85)
ax.axvline(0, color="black", lw=1)
ax.set_xlabel("log10( reported compute / 6ND )")
ax.set_ylabel("models")
plt.show()

print(f"{len(lang)} language models; median ratio {10 ** ratio.median():.1f}x; "
      f"{(ratio.abs() <= np.log10(3)).mean():.0%} within a factor of 3, "
      f"{(ratio.abs() <= 1).mean():.0%} within a factor of 10")

The typical language model reports about three times more compute than \(6ND\) predicts, and only about half land within 10× of it. Several causes are known: training for more than one epoch, dataset sizes reported in words or documents rather than tokens, mixture-of-experts models where only a fraction of \(N\) is active per token, and plain uncertainty in the estimates. \(6ND\) is a useful feature, not a substitute for the label.

A leakage-aware model of training compute

Task. Predict log10(compute) from information that is public when a model is announced: parameters, dataset size, the \(6ND\) estimate, publication year, domain, organisation type, country and whether weights are open. The error is measured in orders of magnitude, so an MAE of 1.0 means a typical miss of 10×.

Leakage. Two columns look useful and must be dropped. Frontier model is defined by Epoch as being among the top models by training compute. Training compute cost is computed from training compute. Either one would give a great test score and a useless model.

Validation. The split is by time. The model trains on everything published before 2023 and is tested on 2023 onwards. A random split would let it learn from 2025 models when predicting 2022 ones, and deployment never offers that luxury. Hyperparameters are tuned with TimeSeriesSplit inside the training period only.

def build_features(df):
    X = pd.DataFrame(index=df.index)
    X["log_params"] = np.log10(df["params"])
    X["log_tokens"] = np.log10(df["tokens"])
    X["log_6nd"] = np.log10(6 * df["params"] * df["tokens"])
    X["year"] = df["year"]
    X["domain"] = df["Domain"].str.split(",").str[0]
    X["org_type"] = df["Organization categorization"].str.split(",").str[0].fillna("Unknown")
    X["country"] = df["Country (of organization)"].str.split(",").str[0].fillna("Unknown")
    X["open_weights"] = df["Open model weights?"].fillna("Unknown")
    return X

numeric = ["log_params", "log_tokens", "log_6nd", "year"]
categorical = ["domain", "org_type", "country", "open_weights"]

dl = dl.sort_values("date")
X, y = build_features(dl), np.log10(dl["compute"])
train, test = dl["year"] < 2023, dl["year"] >= 2023
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = X[train], X[test], y[train], y[test]

Why trees need help here. Gradient-boosted trees predict with piecewise constants, so they cannot extrapolate. For any year after 2022 they answer as if it were 2022. The target, however, grows by roughly two orders of magnitude a year. The fix is to detrend. A linear regression on year captures the trend, and the trees model only what is left. Detrended below wraps any scikit-learn regressor this way, so it can be tuned with GridSearchCV like any other.

class Detrended(BaseEstimator, RegressorMixin):
    """Fit a linear trend on `year`, then fit `estimator` to the residuals."""

    def __init__(self, estimator):
        self.estimator = estimator

    def fit(self, X, y):
        self.trend_ = LinearRegression().fit(X[["year"]], y)
        self.estimator_ = clone(self.estimator).fit(X, y - self.trend_.predict(X[["year"]]))
        return self

    def predict(self, X):
        return self.trend_.predict(X[["year"]]) + self.estimator_.predict(X)

I compare five models. Two are baselines: the training median, and a trend-only regression on year. Ridge regression uses median imputation, missingness indicators and one-hot categories. Gradient boosting is fitted once on the raw target and once detrended. Boosting handles missing values natively and is tuned with time-series cross-validation.

linear_prep = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", make_pipeline(SimpleImputer(strategy="median", add_indicator=True), StandardScaler()), numeric),
    ("cat", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore", min_frequency=5), categorical),
])
tree_prep = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", "passthrough", numeric),
    ("cat", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore", min_frequency=5, sparse_output=False), categorical),
])

def gbm():
    return make_pipeline(tree_prep, HistGradientBoostingRegressor(loss="absolute_error", random_state=0))

grid = {
    "histgradientboostingregressor__max_depth": [2, 3, None],
    "histgradientboostingregressor__learning_rate": [0.03, 0.1],
    "histgradientboostingregressor__max_iter": [200, 400],
}
cv = TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5)
search_raw = GridSearchCV(gbm(), grid, cv=cv, scoring="neg_mean_absolute_error").fit(X_train, y_train)
search_detrended = GridSearchCV(
    Detrended(gbm()), {f"estimator__{k}": v for k, v in grid.items()},
    cv=cv, scoring="neg_mean_absolute_error",
).fit(X_train, y_train)

fitted = {

    "Trend-only baseline": Detrended(DummyRegressor(strategy="constant", constant=0)).fit(X_train, y_train),
    "Ridge regression": make_pipeline(linear_prep, RidgeCV(alphas=np.logspace(-2, 3, 20))).fit(X_train, y_train),
    "Gradient boosting, raw target": search_raw.best_estimator_,

The test set mixes two very different populations. Most models report their parameter count. A minority do not, and those are mostly large closed models. So the scores are split by that.

known = X_test["log_params"].notna()

def score(name, model):
    err = np.abs(y_test - model.predict(X_test))
    return {"model": name,
            "MAE, all": round(err.mean(), 2),
            f"MAE, params known (n={known.sum()})": round(err[known].mean(), 2),
            f"MAE, params missing (n={(~known).sum()})": round(err[~known].mean(), 2),
            "within 10x": f"{(err <= 1).mean():.0%}"}

pd.DataFrame([score(name, m) for name, m in fitted.items()])

Both baselines matter. The median is useless because compute keeps growing, and the trend on its own does much better. Raw boosting comes out worse than ridge regression because it cannot extrapolate. Detrending fixes that. It gives the best overall score and the smallest error on the hard rows, and it ties ridge where parameters are known. The gap between the two MAE columns is the main result. When the parameter count is public, a typical miss is well under an order of magnitude. When it is hidden, the model misses by two or more.

What the model relies on

Permutation importance on the test set measures how much the error grows when one feature is shuffled. That shows what the model needs on data it has not seen, which the impurity scores computed during training cannot show.

imp = permutation_importance(best, X_test, y_test, scoring="neg_mean_absolute_error",
                             n_repeats=30, random_state=0)
order = np.argsort(imp.importances_mean)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 3.8))
ax.barh(np.array(X_test.columns)[order], imp.importances_mean[order],
        xerr=imp.importances_std[order], color="#1b6ca8", alpha=0.85)
ax.set_xlabel("Increase in test MAE (log10 FLOP)")
plt.show()

Parameters and year carry almost all of the signal. The surprise is that log_tokens and log_6nd have negative importance. Shuffling them makes the test error smaller. The model learned how dataset size related to compute before 2023, and that relationship changed afterwards. Recent models are routinely trained on far more tokens per parameter than their predecessors, and the data-size field mixes tokens, words and images across domains. That is a concrete case of distribution shift, and it can be tested directly: refit the same pipeline without the two features and see whether the test error drops.

data_features = ["log_tokens", "log_6nd"]

The two scores disagree, and that disagreement is the signature of the shift. Inside the training period, the dataset-size features help (CV MAE 1.07 vs 1.12). On 2023+ models they hurt (test MAE 1.03 vs 0.92).

This table is the honest version of feature selection. Cross-validation inside the training period is the only score that can pick features, because choosing by test score would leak the test set into the model. Test MAE is shown here to diagnose the shift, not to make the choice. I keep the full model as the main result, because it is the one the training-period evidence supports.

Errors grow steadily from “Confident” to “Speculative” labels. Part of what looks like model error is noise in the target itself.

The largest misses fail in two opposite directions, and both come from the same cause. The year trend is a strong prior, and the model falls back on it when the size information is weak or missing.

  • Under-prediction of flagship closed models (GPT-4.5, Gemini 1.0 Ultra and others). Their parameter count is not disclosed. This is missing not at random: labs withhold size most often for their largest models, so median imputation plus a missingness flag cannot recover information that was hidden because it was large.
  • Over-prediction of small specialised models (a peptide model, a 57-thousand-parameter drone-racing controller, an audio model). Being published after 2023 pulls the prediction toward the frontier, even when the model is tiny.

Doing better would take signals from outside this table, such as API pricing, context length or benchmark scores for closed models, rather than a fancier algorithm.

What I found

  • Training compute for notable models doubles about every 6 months, and for language models about every 5. The bootstrap intervals are tight. Per-chip hardware in my memory-wall post doubles about every 19 months, so most of the growth comes from larger clusters and longer runs, not faster chips.
  • \(C \approx 6ND\) is a rough guide, not a law. The typical language model reports about 3× more compute than it predicts, and only about half land within 10×.
  • Model choice has to match the extrapolation problem. With a time-based split, raw gradient boosting loses to ridge regression because trees cannot extrapolate. Detrending turns it into the best model.
  • The data-size features drifted. They help in cross-validation before 2023 and hurt afterwards. Permutation importance on held-out data caught this, and an ablation confirmed it.
  • Missing data is the real ceiling. When the parameter count is public, the typical miss is well under 10×, and on rows where \(6ND\) exists the model beats the rule alone (MAE 0.74 vs 1.08). For flagship closed models it does not, and the reason is why the data is missing, not the choice of algorithm.

Caveats. The “notable” filter is Epoch’s editorial choice, so this is not a random sample of all models. Many compute labels are themselves estimates, some built from \(6ND\), which flatters the physics features. Speculative labels are the hardest to predict. Weighting training rows by label confidence is a natural next step.
